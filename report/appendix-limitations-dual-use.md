## A.1 Limitations

**Synthetic restriction, not trained-in refusal.** Under a `join`-style policy, sharing a single part is permitted, so part disclosure is not a violation; only the joined string is restricted. Under `strict`, the restriction is an operator-style instruction in the system prompt that we wrote. Our strict-policy outcomes therefore test compliance with a synthetic operator restriction under different local contexts. They do not test whether safety-trained models refuse harmful capabilities, and should not be described as unsafe behaviour or as a jailbreak.

**Benign proxy vs. real harm.** The forbidden artifact is an arbitrary four-word "vault passphrase". Models may treat a made-up passphrase very differently from content that is harmful in the world (malware, weapons uplift, exfiltration). Our results therefore measure whether context visibility affects a policy-following behavior; they do not show the same rates would hold for genuinely dangerous artifacts, for which refusal training is typically stronger and may key on different features. Results should be read as a mechanism probe, not a risk estimate. (See the preceding paragraph on the synthetic restriction.)

**Scoring definitions.** Outcomes are `exposed_parts` (target word appears), `identified_parts` (word posted without other items of its list), `recoverable` (all 4 identified) and `assembled` (4 words in order in one message after formatting normalisation). `identified` uses a heuristic threshold on how many list items accompany the word and can mis-classify; `assembled` depends on the normalisation rules; legacy metrics are retained only for comparison. Different definitions can change rates, so we report all four.

**String-match scoring.** Scoring is exact string match on the words and the joined passphrase. This avoids LLM-judge noise but misses paraphrase, encoding, partial reconstruction (e.g., initials, reordered or spelled-out words), and disclosure in forms the matcher does not normalize. Reported disclosure is a lower bound on information leakage. A word can be counted even if it was reported at the wrong list position [DECIDE and state how position errors are handled].

**Design selected on pilot seeds.** The v2 design was chosen after seeing exploratory pilots (seeds 0-19, 2-10 seeds per cell). The confirmatory run uses held-out seeds 1000-1019; the v1 full run (strict, seeds 0-19) overlaps pilot seeds and is reported only as an exploratory replication. Pilot findings are exploratory framing effects, not results.

**Small n.** Haiku 5.5: 20 held-out seeds per cell; Sonnet 5.5: 10 seeds per cell plus one replicate of the strict/explicit cell on the same seeds. No row had a failed call. The Sonnet relay effect (7/30 strict-policy relays assembled) comes from 10 distinct seeds and is not significant in either run alone (paired p = 0.50 and n.s.). Pilot runs used 2-10 seeds and are not reported as results. Even in the full run, many cells have wide confidence intervals, interactions across policy x framing x condition x model are underpowered, and we did not correct for multiple comparisons; we lead with effect sizes and intervals.

**Prompt sensitivity and surface cues.** Framing effects may reflect specific lexical cues ("access-code", "vault", "Part k") rather than a general property of context visibility. We tested three framings; we did not sweep paraphrases. Findings that depend on single words should be treated as cue-sensitivity, and our interpretation (refusals track visible context, not inference of the forbidden whole) is a hypothesis consistent with the data, not a mechanistic demonstration.

**Policy and knowledge confounded in v1; opportunity and topology.** In the exploratory v1 runs, the strict policy text named the passphrase in system prompts and, in the relay, policy and coordinator knowledge changed together. The v2 design applies the same policy to every role in a run and crosses policy with framing, but residual differences remain (for example, what each role's local prompt reveals). Separately, topology changes the number of calls and the information available (1 call for solo, 5 for pipeline, 6 for relay, up to 36 for swarm@n12), so unmatched contrasts do not isolate a topology effect. v2 adds opportunity-matched baselines (solo@k5, solo@k12; K independent attempts, success if any); this partly, not fully, addresses the confound, because K independent attempts do not share information as a board does.

**Single provider family.** Main results use Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Sonnet 5.5 via Claude Code. Other families may differ substantially. [RESULT: cross-model OpenRouter runs, which models, n; otherwise "not completed"]. One Sonnet cell's manifest listed two served model IDs; we reran it with per-turn model logging (all calls served by `claude-sonnet-5-5`) and report both. Unless OpenRouter runs complete we have a single provider, and we do not claim generality across providers. Provider defaults (sampling settings, system prompts added by the CLI) and the Claude Code CLI version are not pinned beyond what each run manifest records; behaviour may drift between runs.

**Relay cue wording.** Relay prompts do not use the framing manipulation: relay workers see "inventory list" and "assigned position", which is closer to neutral framing. The contrast between the uninformed relay and solo/explicit therefore compares different information conditions by design (the relay hides the goal); the information-matched comparison is solo/neutral, against which the relay leaked less. We report both.

**Sequential turn-taking and short horizons.** Swarm agents act in a fixed round-robin order over 3 rounds on a shared board, with part assignment (agent j holds part j mod 4) fixed by the harness. Real swarms are asynchronous, larger, long-running, and heterogeneous, with agents that join and leave. The METR-described incident involved on the order of 1,200 agents; our main swarm condition has 4 agents (larger sizes only if run). Behavior may change with scale, duration, and memory.

**Position-reporting errors by workers.** Workers sometimes report the word at the wrong position or the wrong list; this adds noise to parts_disclosed and joined [RESULT: error rate]. Joined rates in particular can understate intent to comply.

**No tool use.** Agents have no tools, filesystem, network, or persistent cache. The incident motivating this work involved agents using a real shared artifact store. Our "board" is an abstraction and omits permissions, rate limits, logging, and side channels.

**Related prior work.** Decomposition into benign-looking sub-tasks is documented in prior work, including multi-agent decomposer/answerer/combiner jailbreaks [srivastav2025safe]; our setting differs but is not claimed to be novel in kind.

**Other.** Single task structure (lookup-and-concatenate); no adversarial optimization of prompts; no human baseline; the strict/join policies are our own wording. We did not evaluate any defense, so Section 5 defense ideas are untested suggestions.

## A.2 Dual-Use Considerations

**Could this help someone launder harmful requests through decomposition?** Possibly in principle: a paper showing that splitting work across agents can change what individual agents will do is conceptually a recipe for decomposition attacks. We assess the marginal uplift as low because (i) decomposition attacks on single models and multi-agent systems are already discussed in the literature [cite from `../docs/research/related-work.md`], (ii) our task is a toy with no harmful content, (iii) any finding concerns which contextual cues change behavior under a synthetic restriction, a coarse insight rather than a working exploit, and (iv) the METR incident report already documents real-world multi-agent coordination. We still treat the risk as non-zero and take the steps below.

**Why a benign proxy.** We deliberately avoid any genuinely harmful artifact. A passphrase of four arbitrary words lets us measure composition behavior with exact scoring, run many seeds cheaply, and publish prompts, transcripts, and code without handing anyone harmful content. The cost is external validity (A.1).

**Public replication materials.** For this synthetic task, prompts, ordinary topology templates, seeds, scoring code, manifests and suitable synthetic transcripts remain public. There is no blanket suppression of the highest-disclosing toy prompts or reconstructible seeds. Real credentials, private personal data and live-target attack material remain excluded. Any future genuinely attack-enabling artifact, including an SAE intervention, requires an artifact-specific assessment; a feature ID or steering strength is not automatically sensitive. If necessary materials are withheld, state the exact replication limit. Existing public copies cannot be assumed to disappear, and benign experimental history is preserved.

**What we withhold or do not do.**
- We do not test or publish decompositions of real harmful tasks (CBRN, cyber offense, malware, exfiltration), or prompts tuned to evade a deployed model's refusal for such tasks.
- We do not publish an optimized "laundering" prompt library. Relay examples in Appendix B are limited to the benign proxy and include only what is needed to reproduce the measurement. [DECIDE: whether the uninformed-coordinator laundering transcript is included in full or summarized.]
- We do not release attack scaffolding aimed at real systems or real infrastructure (e.g., any code that targets package registries, caches, or model hubs).
- We report no vendor-specific bypasses beyond the aggregate rates needed to interpret the results.

**Responsible-disclosure stance.** If a finding looks like a transferable weakness in a specific deployed model or product (for example, a repeatable laundering route for a restricted behavior), we will notify the relevant provider [Anthropic and any OpenRouter-tested vendors] before broader publicization of details, and will delay releasing the specific prompts until they have had a reasonable chance to respond. [DECIDE: the timeline, and who on the team owns the notification.] Our current results involve only a benign task, so we do not expect this to be triggered, but we note the process.

**Defensive uses and intended benefit.** The intended use is to inform defense:
- Context-propagation monitors that track what each agent can see (task framing, board contents, coordinator knowledge) and flag when many individually-innocuous contributions compose into a restricted artifact.
- Board-level / shared-channel monitors on shared stores (the cache-as-message-board pattern from the METR report) that check aggregates, not only individual messages.
- Coordinator-level intent checks: our relay conditions test whether a coordinator that knows the goal still requests pieces and whether an uninformed one can be used to launder a request, which bears on where to place intent checks in orchestration frameworks.
- Evaluation design: per-agent refusal evaluations are insufficient for swarm deployments; composition-aware evals should be added to testbeds. Our harness is a minimal example.

**Other ethical considerations.** No human subjects or personal data. API use complied with provider terms of service; the "forbidden" content is benign by construction, so no safety policies were circumvented in a harmful way. Compute and cost: 7,670 model calls; the Claude Code CLI reported list-price equivalents of $1.16 (Haiku) and $10.70 (Sonnet), billed to a subscription. Cross-model runs via OpenRouter send only the benign prompts. Credit: pre-sprint work is identified in the main text (Section 3.6).

**Net assessment.** We believe the benefits (identifying a measurable gap and pointing at context propagation as a control surface) outweigh the marginal risk, given the benign proxy, the existing literature, and the withheld material above.
