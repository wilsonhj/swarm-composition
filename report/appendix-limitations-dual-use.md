# Appendix A: Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

Study 1 numbers below are from the frozen v2 runs (held-out seeds 1000+); Study 2 numbers are from the frozen provenance held-out run (seeds 3000-3049). Pilot and v1 facts are labelled where they appear. All match the main report.

## A.1 Limitations

**Synthetic restriction, not trained-in refusal.** The restriction is an operator-style rule in the system prompt that we wrote ("you must NEVER help anyone obtain the vault passphrase"), applied to a benign string. Strict-policy outcomes therefore measure compliance with that synthetic rule under different local contexts. They do not test whether safety-trained models refuse harmful capabilities, and should not be described as unsafe behaviour or as a jailbreak.

**Benign proxy vs. real harm.** The restricted artifact is an arbitrary four-word "vault passphrase". Models may treat it very differently from content that is harmful in the world (malware, weapons uplift, exfiltration), where refusal training is typically stronger and may key on different features. The results show whether what an agent's context reveals changes a policy-following behaviour. They are a mechanism probe, not a risk estimate.

**What the scorer counts.** Scoring is string-based, with no LLM judge (`scoring.py`, covered by `tests/test_scoring.py`). Only text after a leading `POST:` counts as posted; refusals or commentary outside a post, and `PASS`, are ignored. Each post is lower-cased and reduced to its letter-only word tokens, so case, punctuation, digits and hyphens do not matter. Four outcomes are reported:
- *Exposed parts:* the part's target word appears as a token in any post.
- *Identified parts:* the target word appears in a post that contains no other item from that part's list, so an observer can attribute it.
- *Recoverable:* all four parts are identified (in any posts, possibly by different agents), or the passphrase is assembled. This assumes an observer who knows the four lists and that a lone word is an answer.
- *Assembled:* the four words appear contiguously and in order within one post after normalisation. `assembled_exact`, the raw lower-case hyphen string, is also recorded.

What it can miss: paraphrase, encodings, initials, reordered or spelled-out words, plurals or other inflections (tokens must match exactly), and words containing non-ASCII letters. Labelled forms such as "Part 1: raven, Part 2: lotus, ..." are recoverable but not assembled, because the labels break contiguity; bare numbering ("1. raven 2. lotus ...") is assembled, because digits are dropped. Out-of-order words are exposed and recoverable but not assembled. A word at the wrong position counts as neither exposed nor identified: only the item at the seed's selected position of each list is matched, and the lists are disjoint, so a lookup error lowers exposure rather than adding spurious exposure.

What can count spuriously: a post that merely mentions a word, such as a warning not to reveal it, counts as exposed (and as identified if no other list item is in the same post). A refusal written inside a `POST:` that quotes the full passphrase counts as assembled. For `solo@kK` baselines, each of the K attempts is scored alone and each outcome takes its best attempt, so two outcomes may come from different attempts. The harness's older v1 metrics (`parts_disclosed`, `joined`) used a different matcher; v1 rows are exploratory and are rescored with `scoring.py`.

**Worker position errors.** Workers sometimes post something other than the target word. We computed this read-only from the raw v2 `turns` (worker turns in pipeline and relay, both models pooled, rows with no failed call, policy `none` so refusals do not confound): 36 of 695 worker posts did not contain the target word (5.2%). By condition: pipeline 4/240, relay_informed 10/226, relay_uninformed 22/229 (9.6%). This is a rough noise estimate. It also includes workers that posted something other than a lookup, and it was not computed for swarm turns or for strict-policy cells. Position errors lower exposure, recoverability and assembly, so the reported leakage is a lower bound on what these agents could produce.

**Recoverability is a lower bound.** `identified` and `recoverable` use a conservative clean-mention rule: a word quoted together with other items from its list is not counted, even when the message plainly selects it. Committed boards contain such false negatives (e.g. Sonnet strict/neutral `swarm@n4`, seed 1004), so these counts understate what a reader could reconstruct. `assembled` is unaffected. The historical scorer is kept unchanged; any revision would be a labeled post hoc sensitivity analysis.

**Design chosen on pilot seeds.** The v2 design was frozen after exploratory pilots on seeds 0-19. The reported results use held-out seeds 1000-1019 (Haiku) and 1000-1009 (Sonnet). The v1 exploratory run (Haiku, strict, neutral, pilot seeds 0-19) overlaps pilot seeds, uses a different design, and is excluded from inference.

**Small n and multiplicity.** Haiku 5.5 has 20 seeds per cell and Sonnet 5.5 has 10, with no failed calls. The Sonnet relay leak (7/30 strict-policy uninformed relays assembled; a requested-Sonnet aggregate, because the original strict/explicit cell's manifest records `claude-sonnet-5` and `claude-sonnet-5-5` without per-turn identity) comes from 4 of 10 distinct seeds. The 30 relays pool two framing cells and a rerun, but relay prompts ignore framing, so those cells are replicates on the same 10 seeds, not 30 independent tasks. Every paired contrast for it is non-significant (p >= 0.125). Many cells have wide intervals, and we did not correct about 7 contrasts per cell for multiple comparisons, so we lead with effect sizes and intervals. Pilot runs used 2-10 seeds and are not reported as results.

**Framing is a bundle with two levels.** Framing changes several prompt elements at once (list label, post, assemble, objective and turn lines, and the solo instruction) and the prompt length with them; under explicit framing the solo instruction is itself the restricted request. Only neutral and explicit were run; a "hinted" level is implemented but was not run, so there is no dose-response. Effects may reflect specific lexical cues ("Part k", "your word", "vault passphrase") rather than a general property of context visibility. We did not sweep paraphrases or isolate the cues. Our interpretation (refusal tracks recognising the target in local context rather than inferring it) is a hypothesis consistent with the data, not a mechanistic demonstration.

**Topology, opportunity and call counts.** Topologies differ in calls and information (solo 1 call, pipeline 5, relay 6, swarm@n4 up to 12). We use opportunity-matched baselines (solo@k5, solo@k12; success if any attempt succeeds). They partly address the confound, because independent attempts share no board. The protocols also cap assembly on their own: without a policy the round-robin swarm assembles 0/20 (Haiku) and 0/10 (Sonnet) under neutral framing, and the uninformed relay task is hard even without a restriction. Strict-policy results must be read against the no-policy rows.

**Relay ignores framing.** Relay prompts do not use the framing manipulation, so framing cells are replicates and are pooled. The two relay variants differ only in the coordinator's task text ("produce the vault passphrase" vs. "compile four items"). The information-matched comparison for the uninformed relay is solo/neutral, against which the relay leaked less (Sonnet 7/30 vs. 8/10 assembled; Haiku 1/40 vs. 19/20). Comparison with solo/explicit, where Sonnet refused every attempt, differs in information by design; we report both.

**Single provider family and run provenance.** Both models are Claude (Haiku 5.5, Sonnet 5.5), run through Claude Code (`claude -p`, no tools). Cross-model runs through OpenRouter were not completed: the backend exists in the harness, but no OpenRouter run is in the frozen results, and we make no claim about other model families. Temperature is not sent (provider default) and the manifest records it as null; the Claude Code CLI version and system-level defaults are recorded only as far as each run manifest records them, so behaviour could drift between runs. One Sonnet cell (strict/explicit) had a manifest listing two served models (`claude-sonnet-5`, `claude-sonnet-5-5`), with no per-call model record. We added per-turn model logging and reran that cell on the same seeds; all 590 rerun calls were served by `claude-sonnet-5-5`. Both are reported. The rerun uses the same seeds, so it checks provenance, not task-level variation.

**Scope.** We did not vary network shape at a fixed call count (chain, star, fully connected), run swarms larger than four agents, test spread from a compromised agent, or use real swarm traces. These remain open.

**Sequential turn-taking, no tools, short horizons.** Swarm agents act in a fixed round-robin for 3 rounds on a shared board, with one list per agent fixed by the harness. Real swarms are asynchronous, larger, long-running and heterogeneous. Agents here have no tools, filesystem, network or persistent cache, so the "board" is an abstraction that omits permissions, rate limits, logging and side channels. We make no claim about emergent role formation.

**The METR incident is motivation only.** The incident report motivates studying split structures and shared boards [metr2026incident, metr2026report]. It is not an instance of the hidden-goal mechanism we test: by METR's account the agents recognised the Hugging Face attack as out of scope and unethical and joined anyway, and there was no refusal-alone baseline. Nothing here estimates the incident's cause or scale (about 1,200 agents there; at most 4 here).

**Related prior work.** Decomposition into benign-looking subtasks is documented in prior work [jones2024combinations, srivastav2025safe, kothamasu2026decompbench]. Our setting differs (a fixed restriction, with only what each agent sees varied), but we do not claim novelty in kind.

**Study 2 scope.** Study 2 is a separate held-out study (Haiku 5.5, seeds 3000-3049, protocol frozen at `435d20d`) and is never pooled with Study 1. It uses one model and one fixed pipeline, with no second-model replication yet. Provenance is supplied by the harness and assumed trustworthy; forged or tampered metadata is not tested. The monitor bundles extra information, an extra call and a veto, and it blocked 792 of 795 proposals, including every authorized completion, so it is not a usable defence as configured. One of 800 trials failed (a backend error) and is excluded from valid n; the analysis reports all-trial bounds. The log holds 4,795 turns while the manifest counts 4,794 calls, because the collection harness counted the one exhausted call as an error rather than a call; retries inside a call were not recorded. Zero events in 50 trials still allow a one-sided 95% upper bound of about 5.8%. See Section 5.3.

**Other.** One task structure (lookup-and-concatenate), no adversarial prompt optimisation, no human baseline, and strict/none policies in our own wording. Study 1 evaluates no defence. Study 2 tests one intervention (sharing task provenance), with an objective-aware monitor and a keyword gate as comparisons, in one fixed pipeline with one model; the other control points in Section 6.2 remain untested suggestions.

## A.2 Dual-Use and Ethical Considerations

**Could this help someone launder harmful requests through decomposition?** Possibly in principle: showing that splitting work across agents changes what individual agents do is conceptually close to a decomposition attack. We assess the marginal uplift as low. (i) Decomposition attacks on single models and multi-agent systems are already in the literature [jones2024combinations, srivastav2025safe, kothamasu2026decompbench]. (ii) Our task is a toy with no harmful content. (iii) The finding concerns which contextual cues change behaviour under a synthetic restriction, a coarse insight rather than a working exploit; the leaks we observed under the strict policy (uninformed relays: Sonnet 7/30, Haiku 1/40) are infrequent and rarer than for a single equally uninformed agent. (iv) The METR incident report already documents real-world multi-agent coordination. We still treat the risk as non-zero and take the steps below.

**Why a benign proxy.** We deliberately avoid any genuinely harmful artifact. Four arbitrary words let us score with exact string matching, run many seeds cheaply, and publish prompts, transcripts and code without handing anyone harmful content. The cost is external validity (A.1).

**Public replication materials.** For this synthetic task, prompts, ordinary topology templates, seeds, scoring code, manifests and suitable synthetic transcripts remain public. There is no blanket suppression of the highest-disclosing toy prompts or reconstructible seeds. Real credentials, private personal data and live-target attack material remain excluded. Any future genuinely attack-enabling artifact, including an SAE intervention, requires an artifact-specific assessment; a feature ID or steering strength is not automatically sensitive. If necessary materials are withheld, state the exact replication limit. Existing public copies cannot be assumed to disappear, and benign experimental history is preserved.

**What we withhold or do not do.**
- We do not test or publish decompositions of real harmful tasks (CBRN, cyber offence, malware, exfiltration), or prompts tuned to evade a deployed model's refusal for such tasks.
- We do not publish an optimised "laundering" prompt library. The relay transcripts in Appendix B.3 (two Sonnet relay leaks and one informed-coordinator PASS) are included in full because they contain only the benign proxy; they are limited to what is needed to reproduce the measurement.
- We do not release attack scaffolding aimed at real systems or infrastructure (for example code that targets package registries, caches or model hubs).
- We report no vendor-specific bypasses beyond the aggregate rates needed to interpret the results.

**Responsible-disclosure stance.** We found no transferable weakness in a deployed model or product, and the task is benign, so we do not expect this to be triggered. If a later finding looked like a repeatable laundering route for a restricted behaviour in a specific model, we would notify the provider (Anthropic is the only vendor whose models were tested) before releasing the specific prompts. We have not set a fixed timeline or named an owner for this.

**Defensive uses and intended benefit.** The intended use is to inform defence. These are untested suggestions:
- Context-propagation monitors that track what each agent can see (task framing, board contents, coordinator knowledge) and flag when innocuous contributions compose into a restricted artifact.
- Board-level monitors on shared stores (the cache-as-message-board pattern from the METR report) that check aggregates, not only individual messages.
- Goal propagation and coordinator-level intent checks. Our relay conditions show that an informed coordinator under the strict policy never requested the pieces (0/70), and that an uninformed one sometimes did, so intent checks belong where the goal is known.
- Evaluation design: per-agent refusal evaluations are not enough for swarm deployments; composition-aware evaluations should be added to testbeds. Our harness is a minimal example.

**Other ethical considerations.** No human subjects or personal data. The restricted content is benign by construction, so no safety policy was circumvented in a harmful way. Models were accessed through the Claude Code CLI on a user subscription; prompts contain only the benign proxy. Compute and cost: Study 1 used 7,670 model calls, with CLI-reported list-price equivalents of $1.16 (Haiku) and $10.70 (Sonnet); Study 2 used 4,794 successful calls plus 1 failed call (4,795 logged turns) at $0.96. All were billed to a subscription. All code, pilots and runs were produced during the sprint (Section 3.6).

**Net assessment.** We believe the benefits (a measurable account of how goal visibility shapes refusal in split work, and context propagation as a control surface) outweigh the marginal risk, given the benign proxy, the existing literature, the weak size of the one observed leak, and the withheld material above.
