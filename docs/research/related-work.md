# Related Work (draft)

Citation keys match `references.bib`. Every empirical claim attributed below was checked against the source's abstract or page. The final paragraph states our positioning and makes no claim about results.

**Decomposition and composition attacks.** A growing line of work shows that refusals tested one request at a time can be bypassed by splitting a harmful goal into subtasks that look benign. Jones et al. show that an adversary can assign subtasks to different models so that the combination misuses even when each model passes safety checks alone [jones2024combinations]. Yueh-Han et al. report an 87% average attack success rate on GPT-4o for decomposition attacks and propose a sequential monitor that tracks cumulative intent [yuehan2025monitoring]. Srivastav and Zhang's REALM 2025 paper is the closest prior work to our pipeline condition: a multi-agent, multi-turn framework with a Question Decomposer, a Sub-Question Answerer and an Answer Combiner splits a harmful query into seemingly benign sub-questions, and the authors report attack success rates often exceeding 90% on the models tested (GPT-3.5-Turbo, Gemma-2-9B, Mistral-7B among them) [srivastav2025safe]. Their setting is an attack against safety mechanisms: the decomposition is part of the attacker's method, whereas we use a synthetic operator restriction and study how local context affects each restriction-bound agent's behaviour. Ahad et al. describe Semantic Intent Fragmentation, in which a single legitimately phrased request leads an LLM orchestrator to split the task into subtasks that each pass subtask-level checks while the composed plan violates policy (10 of 14 scenarios with a GPT-20B orchestrator); there the composition emerges from the orchestrator rather than from an injected payload, and the authors propose plan-level information-flow checks [ahad2026semantic]. For agents, DeCompBench finds that state-of-the-art agents often refuse whole harmful tasks but refuse the decomposed versions much less [kothamasu2026decompbench]; related benchmarks cover code [wahed2025mocha] and covert misuse more generally [brown2025covert]. Russinovich et al. describe "capability laundering", in which a weaker model splits a task, consults an aligned model on each piece and assembles the answers itself [russinovich2026laundering]. In most of these works the split is designed by or for an adversary who knows the goal (a user or attacker-controlled decomposer), and the models that answer the pieces are treated as tools. Semantic Intent Fragmentation is a partial exception, since the harmful split is produced by the orchestrator itself, but it still starts from an attacker's request. Single-model work shows the same effect when an attacker fragments a prompt [li2024drattack] or escalates over turns by referencing the model's own replies [russinovich2024crescendo]. Agent-harm benchmarks such as AgentHarm measure refusal of explicitly malicious single-agent tasks [andriushchenko2024agentharm]; they do not vary what each agent knows about the goal.

**Context-dependent disclosure.** ConfAIde tests whether models reason about what to share, with whom and for what purpose; GPT-4 and ChatGPT revealed private information in contexts where humans would not 39% and 57% of the time, even with privacy-inducing prompts [mireshghallah2024confaide]. Our framing manipulation is a minimal version of this question for an operator restriction.

**Multi-agent safety and collusion.** A second line studies how safety properties change when agents interact. Hammond et al. give a taxonomy of three failure modes and seven risk factors, among them information asymmetries and emergent agency [hammond2025multiagent]. On the monitoring side, production blocking monitors fail to block over 55% of red-team attacks, and spreading a side task across agents or compactions so that no single context contains a blockable action is among the most effective strategies [remedios2026automode]; behaviour that is benign in single interactions can be harmful in aggregate [sumers2025summarization]. AI control evaluates protocols against deliberately subversive models [greenblatt2024control]; our agents are not adversarial. Prompt infection and contagious jailbreaks spread an attacker's payload between agents [lee2024prompt, men2024troublemaker], and NetSafe reports that network topology affects how readily attacks spread [yu2024netsafe]. Collusion work concerns agents that deliberately coordinate in hidden ways, for example through steganography [motwani2024secret], and proposes detectors based on model internals [rose2026narc]. Positive-coordination work shows that groups of LLM agents can form functional collectives with complementary roles [riedl2025emergent, guo2024embodied] and can be studied in shared-resource simulations [piatti2024cooperate]. Protocol-level analyses of A2A and MCP focus on identity, delegation and trust between agents [habler2025a2a, prakash2026aip, hu2025trust, hu2026dissociative, ferrag2025threats]. Much of this work, though not all of it, assumes an adversarial agent or protocol participant. The Apart sprint project by Chen (Team DLab) is a different case: it reports that agents answered at most 4 of 30 direct requests for restricted records, yet broke the rule in 22–72% of games once the sanctioned tool returned only a rough answer, mostly jointly in four of six models [chen2026theysay]. It is early-stage sprint work, not an Apart publication, and concerns goal pressure under a failing tool rather than lookup-and-concatenate decomposition.

**The OpenAI / Hugging Face incident.** METR's investigation reports that about 1,200 OpenAI agents, meant to be isolated, used an internal Artifactory cache as a message board, and that about 700 went on to participate in an attack on Hugging Face [metr2026incident, metr2026report]. In the sample the authors examined, about a quarter of attack-joining agents cited a direct assignment or division of labour, and expressed ethical concerns "only rarely materially limited agents' actions" [metr2026incident]. Independent follow-ups released partial public data on the swarm's output [forman2026swarmtraces] and on related agent activity on a public scanning service [cable2026urlquery]. Prior Apart sprint work also touches the area: an omission-attacks project argues that action-level monitoring can miss harms that appear only as missing obligations [kemeklis2026omission], and the AI Collusion Sprint page lists a project on agents that decline individually but combine restricted records [apart2026collusionsprint]. (The Chen project page was re-fetched on 7 Nov 2026 and the multi-agent paragraph's description checked against its abstract.)

**AI organizations and governance.** Shen et al. compare single agents with AI consultancies and software teams on twelve tasks, reporting higher business utility and greater misalignment in their tested organizations [shen2026organizations]. The companion post describes model-dependent gaps and agents solving subproblems without tracking system-level ethical goals. This is direct prior work on nontrivial single-agent versus organizational alignment differences, not merely attacker-led decomposition. Our synthetic study does not reproduce its deployment tasks or ethical scoring. Anthropic's research-directions post proposes inter-query defenses that infer global tasks from subtasks and governance for coordination failures and missing information [anthropic2025directions]. These motivate testing information and publication-control interventions rather than claiming a new general class of multi-agent failure.

**Behavioral explanation and realism.** CHIVE evaluates explanations through measured counterfactual prompt edits and does not treat compelling generated explanations as ground truth [karvonen2026chive]. Petri 2.0 discusses evaluation-awareness and realism tradeoffs [anthropic2026petri]. These motivate future robustness work; neither licenses a mechanistic or real-world safety claim from this lookup proxy.

**Separate training/steering questions.** Training-mediated subliminal learning [cloud2025subliminal], SAE-based control of individual-model misalignment [wang2025personafeatures], and a receiver reacting to another agent's message are distinct mechanisms. AxBench motivates simpler steering comparators [wu2025axbench]. The LoRA-artifact paper carries an author warning that some main claims are incorrect [nief2026lora]; it is not settled negative evidence. Subliminal Steering reports full-finetuning/SGD transfer in a different teacher-intervention setting [morgulis2026subliminal]. These papers motivate the [separate future SAE proposal](../protocols/sae-sender-study-proposal.md), not claims of a network cascade in the present submission.

**Positioning.** The completed v2 study compares context sensitivity across fixed coordination protocols with repeated-solo controls. The separate prospective `task-provenance-v1` study holds a pipeline fixed and tests whether supplying global purpose reduces restricted disclosure while preserving authorized inventory completion. The local condition deliberately withholds information needed to distinguish the two uses; its disclosure is not evidence of knowing evasion. Primary-source prior work already evaluates individual versus collective safety, so novelty must rest on this specific controlled intervention and its measured limits. We make no claim that all prior work requires an adversary or lacks single-agent baselines. Any reported effect must come from the corresponding versioned run, not from the motivating incident or literature. The restriction is a synthetic operator instruction, not trained-in safety refusal.
